# R14 · Standards, safety and compliance for the roll-to-garment cell

Research report for the Roll-to-Garment project. Date of research: 2026-10-21. Scope: the machinery-safety, functional-safety, electrical, fire, noise, ergonomics, AI-governance, data-protection, garment-quality and labelling rules that the machine guide (ST00–ST10) and the software guide (M01–M14) must follow in the EU and the US. Every claim carries a source number from the Sources section or is marked "est." / "unverified".

## Findings

### F1 · The EU legal frame: Machinery Regulation (EU) 2023/1230

- Regulation (EU) 2023/1230 repeals and replaces the Machinery Directive 2006/42/EC. It applies from **20 January 2027** with no overlap period: until 19 January 2027 only the Directive may be applied, from 20 January 2027 only the Regulation [11][13]. (The originally published OJ text reads "14 January 2027"; the date was corrected to 20 January 2027 by corrigendum, which is why the EUR-Lex summary, DGUV Test and EU-OSHA all state 20 January 2027 [11][12][13][14].) Articles 26–42 (notified bodies) have applied since 20 January 2024 [11].
- Consequence for this project: a cell first placed on the EU market or put into service before 20 January 2027 is CE-marked under the Directive; anything placed on the market from that date must meet the Regulation. Declarations of conformity may reference both texts at once when the product technically meets both, which DGUV Test recommends for products straddling the date [13].
- Conformity assessment: internal production control (self-assessment, Module A) remains available for most machinery, including robot cells, provided the product is not in Annex I [11]. Annex I **Part A** lists six categories that must go through a notified body regardless of standards used; items 5 and 6 are "safety components with fully or partially self-evolving behaviour using machine learning approaches ensuring safety functions" and "machinery that has embedded systems with fully or partially self-evolving behaviour using machine learning approaches ensuring safety functions… in respect only of those systems" [12]. Annex I **Part B** products may be self-assessed only when designed to harmonised standards covering all relevant EHSRs [11].
- Recital 55 limits the third-party requirement for software to systems with "fully or partially self-evolving behaviour using machine learning approaches ensuring safety functions"; it explicitly does not apply to "software incapable of learning or evolving, and programmed only to execute certain automated functions" [12]. Annex II (safety components) now lists "Software ensuring safety functions" (item 18) and the self-evolving ML variant (item 19) [12].
- The Regulation adds EHSRs for cybersecurity ("protection against corruption"), for safety and reliability of control systems including self-evolving behaviour, for autonomous mobile machinery, and allows digital instructions with a paper copy on request [14][34]. Germany's implementing act (MaschinenDG) entered into force on 6 December 2025 [34].
- Design rule derived: keep every safety function (ST09/M10) deterministic and non-learning so the cell stays out of Annex I Part A and can be self-certified under Module A with harmonised standards (see F2–F4). Learned models (M06–M08, M11) may only act through the deterministic safety layer.

What changed in 2025 (from the ISO foreword as quoted by ANSI, and from TÜV Rheinland and SICK summaries) [1][2][3][4][5]:
- Robot **classification into Class I and Class II** for functional-safety requirements. Class I: light, slow, limited-force robots with reduced requirements; Class II: all other industrial robots. A test methodology for maximum force per manipulator for Class I robots is specified [3][4].
- **Annex C of Part 2 lists every safety function** with a classification (mandatory / conditionally required / optional) and assigns each a required performance level per ISO 13849-1, so integrators no longer rely on the generic "PL d, Category 3" default of the 2011 edition [3][5].
- **ISO/TS 15066 incorporated**: collaborative applications (hand guiding, speed and separation monitoring, power and force limiting) and test methods for force/pressure measurement are now in ISO 10218-2:2025; TS 15066 remains valid only as a reference [3][5].
- **Cybersecurity** requirements "to the extent that it applies to industrial robot safety" (unauthorized access, manipulation, data loss) [3][4].
- Operating **mode requirements** (automatic, manual reduced speed, manual high speed) with a lockable mode selector [4]; additional design requirements and clarified functional-safety requirements [4].
- EU-OSHA/ISO note: Part 1 scope excludes service robots, consumer products and lifting people; application-specific hazards (here: knives, needles, heat press, vacuum) are handled in Part 2 application design [1].

Fit: the cell is an "industrial robot application" (ST04 gantry + arm with grippers inside guarded zones), so ISO 10218-2:2025 is the governing C-standard and ANSI/A3 R15.06-2025 its US twin. Collaborative operation is not needed for 100 shirts/shift with one operator outside the guard; the simplest compliant architecture is full perimeter guarding with interlocked access and no collaborative claims (see Recommendation).

### F6 · Sewing-machine safety: ISO 10821:2005 (+Amd 1:2009)

- ISO 10821:2005 "Industrial sewing machines — Safety requirements for sewing machines, units and systems" (Edition 1, 76 pp., CHF 204) was last reviewed and confirmed in 2015 and is currently at ISO stage 90.60 (close of review); it has one amendment, Amd 1:2009 [38]. EN ISO 10821:2005+A1:2009 is the European version listed by the UK HSE among textile-machinery safety standards [41].
- Scope: sewing machines, sewing units (head + stand + drive) and sewing systems for professional use in clothing, knitwear, hosiery etc.; it covers hazards from needles, knife trimmers, belt drives, noise, heat, ergonomics and electricity; **it is explicitly not applicable to integrated sewing systems within the scope of ISO 11161 (integrated manufacturing systems)**, nor to large shuttle embroidery machines (ISO 11111) [38][40][41].
- Amd 1:2009 sets minimum control performance per ISO 13849-1: machines whose hazards originate mainly from stitch-forming elements need at least **PL b**; other industrial sewing machines **PL c**; adds marking/documentation and spare-parts requirements; it also carries noise criteria (80 dB keyword) [39].
- Guarding specifics: belt intake points at the drive pulley must be guarded so fingers cannot reach (Annex E); needle guards / finger deflectors, eye-protection shields against needle break on programmable pattern and bartack machines (Annex D) [39][40].
- Fit: the ST05/ST06 heads are bought-in sewing machines (overlock 504/514, coverstitch 406/602, lockstitch 301) and inherit ISO 10821 compliance from their manufacturer; **the cell as a whole falls outside ISO 10821 and inside ISO 11161 + ISO 10218-2**, so the head-level guards (needle guard, belt guard, eye shield) must be retained even though the heads sit inside the cell guard, because a technician reaches them in manual mode.

### F7 · Lint and combustible-dust fire risk: NFPA 660:2025 and NEC Class III

- NFPA 660 (2025 edition, current) consolidates NFPA 61, 484, 652, 654, 655 and 664 into one combustible-dust standard; effective 6 December 2024; the next revision moved to the Fall 2027 cycle [42][43]. NFPA 652 no longer exists as a separate document; its dust-hazard-analysis (DHA) requirement is now in NFPA 660 [43].
- NFPA/NEC terminology: "combustible dust" includes powders, fines, fibers and flyings; **combustible fibers/flyings** (> 500 µm, can form an explosible mixture) are treated as combustible dust (NEC Class II or Zone 20–22 group IIIB-type installations), whereas **ignitible fibers/flyings** (e.g., cotton lint) "do not present a flash-fire or explosion hazard" but "present a fire hazard when in a layer" and lead to NEC **Class III** locations [44].
- NEC 500.5(D) Class III locations "usually include some parts of rayon, cotton, and other textile mills… clothing manufacturing plants" where easily ignitible fibers/flyings are handled; Division 1 where they are handled/manufactured/used, Division 2 where stored [45]. Whether a given sewing/cutting room is classified is an AHJ/owner determination based on accumulation, not automatic.
- Fit: the cutter (ST02) with its vacuum bed and the overlock trimmers (ST05/ST06) generate lint; the white paper already calls for accessible lint collection, cleaning intervals and blockage detection near moving or hot parts (section 16). Design rules derived: (1) lint extraction at the ST02 vacuum exhaust and at each overlock/coverstitch knife with a filter housing outside the hot/electrical zones; (2) ST08 heat press and any motor/drive enclosures rated for lint (IP5x or Type 12) and kept clear of layer accumulation; (3) DHA per NFPA 660 Chapter 7 at the customer site when fibre handling is significant; (4) the cell must not be marketed as suitable for Class III Division 1 areas unless the electrical equipment is listed for it.

### F8 · Noise

- EU workers' noise directive 2003/10/EC: lower exposure action value 80 dB(A) daily/weekly, upper 85 dB(A), exposure limit 87 dB(A) including hearing-protector attenuation [47].
- OSHA 29 CFR 1910.95: permissible exposure limit 90 dBA (8-h TWA, 5 dB exchange rate), action level 85 dBA TWA triggering a hearing conservation program, 140 dB peak impulsive limit [46].
- ISO 10821 Amd 1:2009 adds noise measurement/declaration criteria for sewing machines (ISO 3744/3747, ISO 11204 referenced) [39].
- Fit: a single operator supervising the cell for 12 h is covered by the 8-h TWA rules; the 12-h shift shortens the allowed level (OSHA Table G-16a allows 12.1 h at 87 dBA, 8 h at 90 dBA) [46]. The cell's noise emission must be declared in the instructions (EU) — see F1a below for the Regulation's thresholds.

### F10 · Functional safety with AI in the loop: ISO/IEC TR 5469:2024 and what follows

- ISO/IEC TR 5469:2024 "Artificial intelligence — Functional safety and AI systems" (Edition 1, 2024-01, 73 pp., CHF 204; JTC 1/SC 42) is a Technical Report, not a certifiable standard. It covers three situations: AI inside a safety function; non-AI safety functions protecting AI-controlled equipment; AI used to develop safety functions [35].
- Its Clause 6 defines **AI technology classes** and **usage levels**: A1 (AI in a safety-relevant system with automated decision-making), A2 (AI in a safety-relevant system without automated decision-making), B1/B2 (AI only as a development tool), C (AI not part of the safety function but with indirect impact), and **D: AI not part of a safety function and with no impact on it "due to sufficient segregation and behaviour control", e.g., separation through a sandbox or hypervisor** [36]. The TR notes that mature functional-safety standards do not treat AI and that some explicitly forbid it, and that continuous model improvement undermines verification because behaviour "progressively moves away from the rigorously tested, ideally deterministic and repeatable behaviour" [36].
- ISO/IEC TS 22440 (functional safety and AI systems, JWG 4 of SC 42 with IEC TC 65/SC 65A, the IEC 61508 committee) is the normative follow-on being developed from TR 5469 [37]; its publication status is checked in F10a.
- Fit: the white paper's architecture (safety path independent of inference and cloud; "AI never commands safety") maps to **Usage Level D**. The design rule is therefore: the safety PLC (M10/ST09) and its I/O are physically and logically segregated from the inference PC (ST10); AI outputs reach actuators only through the deterministic motion controller and can at most request a stop, never inhibit one. Documented this way, the cell needs no AI-specific conformity assessment under the Machinery Regulation (not Annex I Part A) and the AI stack is not a "safety component" (see F1 and F11).

### F8a · Noise emission declaration (EU) and measurement practice

- Regulation 2023/1230 Annex III 1.7.4.2(u) requires the instructions to state: the A-weighted emission sound pressure level at workstations where it exceeds 70 dB(A) (or state that it does not); the peak C-weighted instantaneous value where it exceeds 63 Pa (130 dB re 20 µPa); and the A-weighted sound power level where the emission sound pressure level at workstations exceeds 80 dB(A) [76]. Annex III also requires noise to be reduced to the lowest level at source [76].
- The HSE NOMAD guide for manufacturers: the emission value must be determined with an appropriate test code whether or not the machine is considered noisy; uncertainty K must be given; sound power is typically 10–20 dB above the emission sound pressure level; declared values should use dual-number format (idling / under load) [77]. IFA gives 8–25 dB for the same gap and the same 70 / 80 dB(A) decision table [78].
- For textile machinery, ISO 11111-1:2016 points to the ISO 9902 series as the noise test code and ISO 23771 for noise reduction design [97]. ISO 10821 Amd 1:2009 adds noise measurement criteria for sewing machines [39].
- Measured dB(A) values for industrial overlock and coverstitch heads were not found in a citable source (unknown; the head vendors' declarations from R04 should carry them). Design rule: specify in the ST05/ST06 purchase order that each head's declared LpA and uncertainty be delivered, and measure the cell at FAT.

### F9a · Roll masses and manual-handling numbers

- Roll mass formula: kg = length (m) × GSM × total width (m) ÷ 1,000; e.g., 1,000 m of 300 gsm at 1.50 m total width = 450 kg; tubular lay-flat width must be doubled to get fabric width [80]. T-shirt single jersey is typically 140–180 gsm, medium knits 180–260 gsm [81]; the white paper freezes the first product at 240–300 gsm cotton jersey (white paper section 09).
- Worked example for the cell: 240 gsm × 1.80 m open width (= 90 cm tubular lay-flat) = 432 g per linear metre, so a 25 kg roll is about 58 m and a 50 kg roll about 116 m (arithmetic from [80]; mill roll masses themselves are unverified and must come from the chosen supplier).
- NIOSH lifting equation load constant: 23 kg under ideal conditions; handcart limits 227 kg (four-wheel) and 114 kg (two-wheel) [79]. ISO 11228-1:2021 applies from 3 kg and covers working days up to 12 h [48]. Commercial roll lifters and roll trolleys exist precisely for cutting-room roll transfer [80 context; vendor pages not relied on].
- Design rule: ST01 must accept rolls from a floor-level cradle or a powered roll lifter; a manual two-person lift of a 25–50 kg roll onto a shaft above waist height violates the spirit of ISO 11228-1 for a repetitive 12-h task and should be excluded in the instructions.

### F10a · Status of ISO/IEC TS 22440 (AI functional safety)

- ISO/IEC TS 22440 parts 1 (Requirements), 2 (Guidance) and 3 (Examples of application) are Committee Drafts at stage 30.60 (comment period closed 2026-04-11; project approved 2024-05-06) [71]. No published, certifiable standard for AI inside a safety function exists in October 2026; A3 and MassRobotics describe it as forthcoming [37][71].
- Consequence: the only defensible route today is Usage Level D of TR 5469 (AI segregated from safety functions), which is also what keeps the cell out of Machinery Regulation Annex I Part A.

### F11 · The EU AI Act after the Digital Omnibus on AI (Regulation (EU) 2026/1744)

- Regulation (EU) 2026/1744 of 8 July 2026 (the "Digital Omnibus on AI") amends the AI Act (2024/1689), the aviation regulation and the Machinery Regulation 2023/1230; it was published on 24 July 2026 and entered into force on 27 July 2026 [69][70][33][106].
- New application dates for the high-risk obligations (Chapter III Sections 1–3): **2 December 2027** for stand-alone high-risk systems (Article 6(2), Annex III) and **2 August 2028** for AI that is a safety component of regulated products (Article 6(1), Annex I), instead of 2 August 2026 / 2 August 2027 [69][70][31]. The AI Act's general application date of 2 August 2026 and the Article 50 transparency duties were not deferred [106].
- Machinery: "the products covered by the machinery regulation were exempted from direct applicability of the AI act", and the Commission is empowered to adopt delegated acts under the Machinery Regulation adding health-and-safety requirements for AI systems classified as high-risk [31]; the Omnibus deletes point 1 (machinery) of AI Act Annex I Section A and adds a point to Section B [33]. The EPRS briefing confirms that AI-enabled machinery products "need only comply with the sectoral approach in the Machinery Regulation" [30].
- Safety-component definition narrowed: AI systems used solely for non-safety user assistance, performance optimisation, service efficiency, automation, convenience or **quality control** do not qualify as safety components, whereas AI whose failure or malfunction would endanger health and safety does; a manufacturer is not forced into third-party conformity assessment only because a product contains a high-risk AI system when the sectoral law does not require it [33].
- Fit: the cell's AI (design, pattern, nesting, manipulation skills, inspection) is a quality/productivity function, not a safety component, provided the safety layer is independent (F10). Then neither the AI Act high-risk regime nor the Machinery Regulation's Annex I Part A applies; what remains are the AI Act's transparency duties where applicable, the Machinery Regulation's general EHSRs on control systems and corruption (F16), and good-practice documentation (NIST AI RMF, as the white paper proposes).

### F12 · Data protection: GDPR and the EU Data Act

- GDPR definitions: personal data is any information relating to an identified or identifiable natural person; biometric data is personal data "resulting from specific technical processing relating to the physical, physiological or behavioural characteristics" that "allow or confirm the unique identification" of a person [82]. Customer body measurements and fit photos linked to a name or order are personal data; they become special-category biometric data only when processed to uniquely identify the person (e.g., face recognition), which the cell does not need.
- Art. 5 principles: lawfulness/fairness/transparency, purpose limitation, data minimisation, accuracy, storage limitation, integrity/confidentiality, accountability [83]. Art. 28: a processor (e.g., the cell vendor hosting customer designs) acts only on documented instructions under a written contract and may not engage sub-processors without authorisation [84]. Art. 32: security appropriate to risk, including pseudonymisation and encryption and the ability to ensure ongoing confidentiality, integrity, availability and resilience [85].
- EU Data Act (Regulation (EU) 2023/2854) applies from 12 September 2025 and covers connected products including industrial machinery; users (the factory) have a right to access product and related-service data in a structured, machine-readable format, and products placed on the market after 12 September 2026 must be designed for direct data access by default; prototypes are out of scope; the manufacturer's use of non-personal product data needs a contract with the user [86][87].
- Fit with white paper section 16 (role-based access, encryption, controlled retention, explicit training-data permission, project separation between competing labels, authenticated updates): these map one-to-one onto Art. 5, 28 and 32 and onto the Data Act's contract requirement for the vendor's own use of machine data. Design rules: separate tenant data stores per customer (M02/M14); log every design access; keep training-data use opt-in and recorded; offer a data-export endpoint (Data Act); sign and version firmware/policies (also required by Machinery Regulation 1.1.9, F16).

### F14 · Labelling regulations for the finished T-shirt

- US fibre labelling (Textile Fiber Products Identification Act, 16 CFR Part 303): each product must carry the generic fibre names and percentages by weight, the manufacturer's or marketer's name or FTC registered identification number (RN), and the country of origin; garments with a neck must carry the country-of-origin label at the inside centre of the neck midway between the shoulder seams, and the country-of-origin disclosure must be on the front of the label; fibre content and RN may be on the same or another conspicuous label [57][60].
- US care labelling (16 CFR Part 423): manufacturers and importers must attach a permanent care label with regular care instructions that stays legible for the useful life of the garment [58]. The FTC reopened the rule with a Supplemental Notice of Proposed Rulemaking listed in February 2026 [59]; earlier proposals (2020) considered allowing professional-wet-cleaning instructions and revisiting the rule; the 2026 notice's content is recorded in F20 if obtained, otherwise it is an open question.
- EU fibre labelling (Regulation (EU) No 1007/2011): textile products may be made available only if labelled, marked or accompanied by commercial documents with the fibre composition using the fibre names in its Annex I; the rules also cover non-textile parts of animal origin [61]. The Commission plans to revise the Regulation to add physical and digital labelling specifications and sustainability parameters linked to the ESPR [62]; the ESPR delegated act for textiles (digital product passport) is planned for Q4 2027 [63].
- Fit: the ST07 label station must sew in (or heat-apply) a label carrying fibre content, origin, RN/name and care symbols; the canonical garment record (M02) must hold the label text per market so that the label is generated from data, not typed per order.

### F15 · Integrated manufacturing systems: ISO 11161

- ISO 11161:2007 (+Amd 1:2010) "Safety of machinery — Integrated manufacturing systems — Basic requirements" is current (reviewed and confirmed 2023) but is "expected to be replaced by ISO/FDIS 11161 within the coming months"; the FDIS (stage 50.00) is retitled "Safety of machinery — Integration of machinery into a system — General requirements" [64][66]. It covers only the safety-relevant interconnection of two or more machines: task zones, zone interfaces, access paths, material-flow interfaces, span of control of control devices, IMS modes (normative Annex D in the draft) [64][65].
- Fit: the cell is an IMS by definition (cutter + robot + sewing heads + conveyors under one controller). ISO 11161 governs how the ST09 safety PLC partitions the cell into zones so that one zone can be serviced while others run, and how each e-stop's span of control is marked. Adopt the FDIS structure now and update the reference when the 2026/2027 edition is published.

### F16 · Cybersecurity obligations: Machinery Regulation EHSRs and the Cyber Resilience Act

- Machinery Regulation Annex III 1.1.9 ("protection against corruption") requires that connections and remote access cannot lead to hazardous situations and that safety-related software and data are protected against accidental or intentional interference; 1.2.1 requires control systems to withstand reasonably foreseeable external influences including malicious attempts [96]. Products certified under an EU cybersecurity certification scheme (Cybersecurity Act) get presumption of conformity for 1.1.9/1.2.1 [96]; prEN 50742 is the standard being drafted for protection against corruption, with IEC 62443 and IEC TS 63074 as references [96].
- Cyber Resilience Act (Regulation (EU) 2024/2847): entered into force 10 December 2024; reporting of actively exploited vulnerabilities and severe incidents applies from **11 September 2026** (ENISA single reporting platform open); notification of conformity-assessment bodies from 11 June 2026; full application **11 December 2027** [94][95][108]. Recital 53: products under the Machinery Regulation that are also products with digital elements must meet both sets of requirements, and CRA compliance can facilitate the machinery EHSRs on corruption and control-system reliability [94].
- Fit: the cell (networked PLC, edge PC, cloud link) is a product with digital elements; the vendor must run a vulnerability-handling process now (reporting duties already apply), and from 11 December 2027 CE-mark the product also under the CRA. Design rules: no safety logic reachable from the network; signed firmware and policy updates (white paper section 16); version log of safety-related software (traceability evidence shared between CRA and MR files [96]).

### F17 · US enforcement notes (OSHA)

- OSHA's Technical Manual Section IV Chapter 4 (industrial robot systems) still references ANSI/RIA R15.06-2012 and RIA TR R15.706 and ties maintenance work to 29 CFR 1910.147 (lockout/tagout) and 1910.333 [91]; expect inspectors to use R15.06-2025 as it supersedes 2012 [7].
- OSHA 29 CFR 1910.212 is the general machine-guarding rule (point of operation, ingoing nip points, rotating parts, flying chips) [102]. OSHA's letter of 21 October 2024 states that OSHA does **not** recognise the ANSI B11.0 / Z244.1 "alternative methods" definition as equivalent to lockout/tagout; temporary re-energisation is allowed only for testing/positioning under 1910.147(f)(1) [92]. ANSI/ASSP Z244.1-2024 nevertheless elevates alternative methods to co-equal status with LOTO [93]; a cell sold in the US therefore needs both an energy-isolation procedure (lockable disconnect, pneumatic dump valve) and task-based safeguarding for minor servicing.

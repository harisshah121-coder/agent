How much Canadian public money can we get?

Realistically CAD 1.3–3.5 million of non-repayable money in the first 24 months, plus CAD 0.5–3 million of interest-free loans. With an industry partner on a cluster project, the total can reach CAD 6–8 million. The single biggest piece is the SR&ED tax credit. We only get it as a Canadian-controlled company, so how we take Saudi or US money decides whether we keep it.

Assumptions behind the estimate

  • An Ontario-incorporated Canadian-controlled private corporation (CCPC) doing the R&D in Ontario.
  • Six to eight engineers in years 1–2 at about CAD 120k average salary (estimate), plus prototype materials. That is roughly CAD 1.1–1.6 million of SR&ED-eligible spend per year.
  • The development cell from Build Guide 1 (about USD 259k of parts).
  • Total government assistance on a project is usually capped at 75% of its eligible costs for a for-profit company. An interest-free loan typically counts at half its value toward that cap.

Government money that comes as equity

  • BDC Industrial Innovation Venture Fund II is a $200M fund launched in August 2025. It explicitly targets robotics, automation, applied AI and industrial software. It is the most natural public co-investor in our seed round. [20] BDC's Deep Tech Venture Fund is closed to new investments. [21]
  • Growth VCCI: $1 billion from 2026–27, flowing through private venture funds rather than directly to companies. [22]
  • Provinces: Alberta Innovates can now co-invest equity alongside a private lead. Its grant programs are partly paused for redesign from 29 May 2026. [23] Québec gave Montréal tights maker Sheertex a $35M grant (reported December 2024), the clearest apparel-technology precedent in Canada. [24]

The structure question decides the biggest cheque

The 35% refundable SR&ED credit and Ontario's 8% credit go only to Canadian-controlled private corporations. A company loses that status if non-residents control it, by votes or in practice. That covers Saudi investors with control, and a Canadian subsidiary under a US (Delaware) parent. Without it, SR&ED drops to a 15% non-refundable credit, which is worth nothing before we have taxable profit. [25][26]

On our estimate that is CAD 0.5–1 million a year left on the table. It also weakens our case with IRAP and the regional agencies.

Recommended setup

  1. Canadian parent company that stays Canadian-controlled through the seed. Founders and Canadian investors keep voting control. Foreign investors, Saudi included, take non-controlling stakes, and their side agreements must not hand them control in practice.
  2. A US subsidiary for US sales and US investors who need a US entity. This gives North American investors the US presence they look for without moving R&D control abroad.
  3. If a flip to a US parent comes later, typically at Series A, file the SR&ED claims for the period before the flip first. Credits earned while Canadian-controlled stay with that period.
  4. Get a Canadian tax lawyer to review the cap table before the first cheque. Convertible notes, options and voting agreements can all count as control.

Order to apply in

  1. Now: incorporate as an Ontario CCPC. Book an NRC IRAP advisor call. Start timesheets and an R&D log from day one, which SR&ED requires.
  2. Months 1–3: find a university partner (fabric handling or machine vision) for Mitacs, NSERC Alliance and OCI C2C. Apply for the first IRAP project.
  3. Months 3–6: prepare a FedDev Regional AI Initiative application, as an interest-free loan for the development cell. Line up a uniform or apparel manufacturer as partner for the next NGen or Scale AI call.
  4. End of year 1: file the first SR&ED claim. The refund lands roughly 2–4 months later, which is a meaningful cash injection.
  5. When cells deploy in Ontario: claim the 15% Ontario Made Manufacturing credit on each cell put into production.

Two market facts that affect the pitch

  • Domestic uniform buying is real. An RCMP tender for 60,000 general duty shirts was limited to Canadian goods (bids closed April 2026). [27] Canadian Armed Forces clothing runs through one prime contractor, Logistik Unicorp, on a 20-year contract worth up to $3.7B. That makes Logistik Unicorp a partner target rather than a competitor. [28]
  • A US tariff of up to 50% on Canadian textile and apparel imports was announced for 19 August 2026. Whether it took effect is unconfirmed. [29] It matters if we sell Canadian-made shirts into the US. It does not directly apply to selling machines. It is also why the tariff-response programs above exist.

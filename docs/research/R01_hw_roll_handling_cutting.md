## Findings

Scope note: all cutters below are compared at the cell's working width. The white paper's product is a heavyweight (280 gsm) cotton single-jersey T-shirt [131]; typical open-width jersey rolls are 60–72 in (1.5–1.8 m) wide, so the cutter needs a **1.8 m (70 in) class** working width with conveyor. Zünd calls this the "L" size (1800 mm with material transport) [2]; Eastman offers 72 in and 78 in [33]; Bullmer offers 1800 mm [42]; Kuris EDGE offers 1.60 / 1.75 m [59][60].

### A. Roll handling (ST01): unwind / let-off, cradles, A-frames, loading aids

Key facts from vendors:
- Zünd's own roll-handling range (for G3/S3/D3) comprises passive universal roll-off, passive and **active (motor-driven) shaft roll-off** (rewinds slack at the end of unwinding), the **Center Winder** (rolls up to **100 kg / 220 lb**, driven unwind *and* rewind, "consistent feeding tension ... even challenging textiles advanced without creases") and the **Cradle Feeder 100** (no shaft; damaged cores accepted; rapid rewind) [1][4][5]. Sizes/weights per cutter size are in the roll-off overview PDF [5] (not retrieved; request). Price: not published; request quote.
- Bullmer's apparel configuration uses the **AWV-A unwinding unit with center drive and edge alignment**, optionally with the **Transroll-P** roll magazine for automatic roll change [41]. A used Bullmer **Single-Feeder** (rolls ≤1800 mm wide, ≤70 kg, ≤400 mm dia., roll motor + dancer bar for tension-free roll-off, edge control, cones) was listed by Bullmer at **€8,950 net** [44] — the only hard price found for a cutter-grade knit feeder.
- Eastman sells the **Power Cradle** ("feeding ... from a cradle, in a relaxed or tensionless state with precise edge control") and **Power Feed** 1/2/3-roll driven stands, roll stands, and the A-Frame manual feeder (78 in standard; 8 rolls; 250 lb per bar; 16 in max roll dia.; widths 60–132 in) [37][38][66]. Prices not published (request-a-quote shop) [38].
- Kongsberg's **Motorized Roll Feeder** (sign/graphics heritage but the best-documented spec set): max roll width 3210 mm, max dia. 470 mm, max **300 kg**, dancer-loop control with adjustable weights, **edge correction on the shaft up to ±50 mm**, spreader (brush) roll, 23 m/min, docks to the table with magnets, 220 V/50 Hz or 110 V/60 Hz [64]. Price not published.
- Caron **Vegas-DUO** (Italian unwinder sold with the FASTSEWN system): expansion shaft or **dual motorized roller** unwinding, 100–200 kg rolls, useful widths 180–320 cm, roll dia. 35/40 cm, "electronic control of fabric tension, synchronization with electronic dancer bar or material loop", automatic alignment, handles 170–330 gsm and "stretchy fabrics" [63]. Price not published.
- Other cradle unwinders listed on DirectIndustry: Cutting Trading International **Cradle K3** (narrow- or broad-belt driven), Sodifa **ESCA 528 BT**, Handsaeme A-frames with pneumatic shaft, drive unit and edge control [66].
- Pathfinder's EasyLoader synchronises the feed conveyor to the cutter and claims this "eliminates the traditional dancer bar" for single-layer delivery without stretch [125] — relevant design precedent for our own feed path (synchronised driven nip/conveyor instead of a free dancer).

**Tension control components (for a self-built let-off).** Dancer-arm unwind control is the standard, forgiving method for low-tension webs; the controller trims brake/motor torque to hold the dancer at set-point, and a diameter sensor can compensate inertia across the roll [67]. Mitsubishi's roll-to-roll guide documents the servo-driven unwind + dancer-position loop and the "tension generated by dancer roll weight" architecture that suits knits (tension set by a light dancer mass or low air-cylinder pressure, not by torque) [68]. Double E's controller (24 V DC, dancer or load-cell input, 0–10 V/4–20 mA out, Modbus RS232) is a representative off-the-shelf closed-loop unit [69]. A knit-safe target is a few N across the web: the white paper requires "low tension ... not uncontrolled slack" [131]; no vendor publishes a knit tension number — must be measured (see Open questions).

### A3. Knit relaxation (the "24-hour practice") and relaxing machines

- Practice: knitted fabric must relax 24–48 h before cutting (textile-engineering references: 24–48 h [84]; 100% cotton jersey 180 gsm shrinks 5–7 % length × 3–5 % width if cut unrelaxed; cotton/5 % spandex needs 48–72 h [84]). Oshima (relaxing-machine maker) states 12–24 h natural relaxation, or **6–8 h with steam-assisted relaxing** [78][79]. Svegea and Suntech sell air-flotation relaxers that claim to "eliminate at least 24 h rest" (vendor claim, not independently verified) [81][83].
- The white paper already assumes relaxation may be needed and that "a fast in-cell feeder cannot be assumed to replace all conditioning" [131] — consistent with these sources. Implication for the cell: relaxation is an **offline batch step** (roll in → plaited/relaxed → re-rolled) performed by the operator the day before; it does not belong inside the 12-hour takt.

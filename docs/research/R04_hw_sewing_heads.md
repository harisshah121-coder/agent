### F1. What the T-shirt needs from ST05/ST06 (stitch classes and seam roles)

- Structural knit seams (shoulder, side/sleeve-underarm, sleeve set, neckband attach): overlock 514 (2-needle 4-thread) or 504 (1-needle 3-thread); Coats specifies 14–16 stitches per 3 cm for knitted T-shirt overlock, needle Nm 70–90 [99][100].
- Hems (sleeve, bottom) and neck topstitch/cover: coverstitch 406 (2-needle) or 602 (3-needle with top cover), 14–18 stitches per 3 cm [99][100]; a left-hand fabric trimmer subclass (JUKI H22/H23, Yamato VG3721 "left knife hemming", Jack W4 /08) trims the hem allowance in line with the seam [81][22][28].
- Lockstitch 301 is needed only for labels, decorative topstitch and future woven garments; Coats lists 12–14 stitches per 3 cm with Epic Tex 24/30 [99]. For the premium T-shirt it is an optional head.
- Kansai Special's published T-shirt operation map confirms the industry mapping: neck rib attaching on a 2-needle 4-thread top-feed cylinder-bed overlock (JJT5014GH-01M), shoulder tape on a feed-off-the-arm double chainstitch (SQ7702FB-L-TC2), and bottom hemming on a cylinder-bed bottom-cover automat (NR9803GALK-ABH-4) [89].

### F11. How integrators drive heads from a PLC (four proven patterns)

1. Keep the OEM servo box and drive its I/O (pedal emulation). EFKA boxes accept a digital or analog actuator input and provide "machine running", position and 512-pulse outputs, with in1–i10 inputs and M7–M11 outputs for foot lift, trimmer, puller and stacker sequencing; the C200 compiler lets the box itself run a sequence [57][58]. JUKI SC-922 exposes CN51 (eight 5 V inputs, eight NPN outputs, a 360-pulse rotation output) and the CN39 standing-pedal port by which "the sewing machine can be controlled with external signals" [62]. Ho Hsing i60 offers 7 inputs, 8 solenoid outputs and a 1–360 pulse output [64]. This pattern keeps the OEM trimmer, foot-lift and needle-positioning logic and is the cheapest; speed is a command level, not a position-synchronised axis.
2. Serial parameter interface. Dürkopp Adler DAC ACCESS gives a documented RS232/RS422/RS485 telegram protocol at 115,200 bit/s to read and write stitch length, max speed, foot pressure, trimmer and to start/stop by a simulated pedal value, with push telegrams for I/O and current values [54]. It is the only vendor-documented PLC protocol found; it applies to DA flatbed, cylinder-arm, post-bed and chainstitch classes, not to overlock or coverstitch heads from Japanese makers.
3. Replace the OEM motor with an industrial servo on the cell fieldbus. DLRoSS replaced the Brother FB-N21A overlock motor with a Yaskawa SGM7A motor and SGD7S drive on EtherCAT, and measured the true feed rate with a torque-controlled monitor roller rather than trusting the stitch count [120]. KSL's robot heads synchronise the sewing drive with the robot's additional axes [116]. This gives stitch-level position synchronisation (needed for a robot that must hold the ply tension in phase with the feed dog) but discards the OEM trimmer/foot-lift logic, which must be re-implemented on the PLC with the head's solenoids or air valves.
4. Microcontroller shim in front of the OEM panel. The Siemens/Sewbo work interfaced a JUKI CP-180 panel to a microcontroller that ROS commands for presser foot, trimmer and enable [118]; its factory deployment put start/stop, alarms, e-stop and low-level I/O on a PLC under a ROS orchestration layer [119].

Speed on knits: overlock heads are rated 7,000–8,500 sti/min, but automats and research rigs run far lower: JTR hemming automat up to 5,000 sti/min [80], JUKI DDL-9000C shipped at 4,000 sti/min [41], Racing puller continuous 4,000 sti/min [92], JUKI's noise rating measured at 6,500 sti/min [4]. For a robot-fed seam the feed rate is set by the handling, so plan 2,500–4,500 sti/min on jersey (est., basis: the figures above and the DLRoSS roller rig that ran one stitch per second in its validation test [120]).

### F13. Robot-fed overlock on curling single jersey: what is and is not proven

- Proven commercially, operator-loaded: cylinder-arm overlock and coverstitch heads sew rib bands, necklines and hems on knit tubes with electronic edge guiding, expansion rollers and stackers once a person has loaded the tube (Atlanta 1278-6, 1278-8, 1289; JUKI JTR-MF7923/BHA at 2,400–3,000 hems per 9 h) [87][79]. Anti-curl air devices (Siruba UC, KB301) and floating or walking feet (Yamato AZ8000SD, VG WF1) exist precisely because single jersey curls [35][16][22].
- Proven in research, with caveats: fixture-free robot sewing with closed-loop edge tracking is demonstrated on stiffened woven cotton, denim and polyurethane (seam error about 1.4–3 mm; 90 % success in a factory pilot at 180–360 s per trial) [118][119]; stretch-ratio control of two stretchable plies on a servo-driven overlock is demonstrated for straight seams with the leading ends placed by hand under the presser foot (average error <3 mm over 115 trials) [120]; alignment and flattening assume flat, wrinkle-free plies [121][122].
- Claimed, not evidenced publicly: SoftWear's vision-steered T-shirt workline (third generation due H1 2026) and Silana's fixture-free sewing (90 % automation, 6–9 min per garment) [108][110].
- Not found anywhere: a peer-reviewed or vendor-documented demonstration of a robot picking a curling single-jersey ply from a free state, introducing its lead-in under an overlock presser foot and knife, and sewing a curved seam (neckline or armhole) without a fixture or stiffener. The DLRoSS authors list curved stretch seams as future work [120]. Treat this as unproven and plan the ST05/ST06 rigs accordingly (see Open questions).

Decision 1: ST05 overlock head (structural seams). Selected: JUKI MO-6814S-BE6 class head with a JUKI DD control box variant or, if the engineer's manual does not expose external I/O, the belt-drive MO-6814S head driven by an EFKA AB321A/DC1550 or JUKI SC-922/M51N box whose I/O is documented (F5). Reasoning: 7,000 sti/min, 1:0.6–1:4 differential, 27 kg head, DC×27 needles, well-documented manuals with table drawings, USD 1,550 complete at US resellers, worldwide spares [1][2][46]. Alternates: Pegasus EX5214-M03/333 with PP pneumatic foot pressure and PL foot lift when quotes arrive (better feed control on knits, 7,500 sti/min) [9][11]; Siruba 747N-514M3-24-SCT-DKNQ1-4 for its stepping-motor cutter, foot-lift and feed-dog-height adjustment plus UC anti-curl device at a lower price (USD 1,363 complete for the 757K sibling) [34][35][38]. Risk: none of the Japanese or Taiwanese overlock boxes publish a PLC protocol; expect to drive the head by pedal-emulation I/O or to re-motor it (F11). Buy one head early and strip the control box to verify the I/O before committing.

Decision 2: ST05 coverstitch head (hems, neck topstitch). Selected: JUKI MF-7923-U11-B56/UT51 (cylinder bed, needle and looper trimmer, SC-921B box with documented CN51-type I/O) for tubular sleeve and bottom hems, specified as the H23 hemming subclass with left-hand trimmer [81][85]. Risk: cylinder-bed access and tubular hemming need the rotary roller/expander mechanism the JTR and Atlanta automats use; a flat bed (Jack W4) cannot hem a closed tube.

Decision 3: ST06 collar module head and rib preparation. Selected: a cylinder-arm overlock (JUKI MO-6814S, or Kansai Special JJT5014GH-01M top-feed cylinder bed, which the vendor lists for neck-rib attaching [89]) with a pneumatic expansion-roller loader modelled on Atlanta 1289/1278-6 [87], plus a roll-fed rib metering, cutting and loop-closing unit modelled on Atlanta 1996 [87]. Reasoning: this is the only commercially proven way to distribute rib stretch around a neckline, and it matches the white paper's requirement that the module cut and join the rib itself. Alternate: buy an Atlanta 1996 (quote) as the rib-preparation sub-module and a 1289 loader, then automate the body loading with ST04. Risk: Atlanta's documentation is from 2017 and the units are operator-loaded; pricing and current availability are unverified; the loader-to-overlock lead-in on curling jersey is the unproven step (F13).

Decision 4: lockstitch. Selected: none in the first cell; add a JUKI DDL-9000C-SMS (USD 1,980–2,000 complete, 400 W DD, digital feed, documented table drawing) [40][45] only if labels or decorative topstitch are in the released T-shirt spec. Alternate: Brother S-7300A (34.5 kg, electronic feed) [47]. Heavy-duty reference: Dürkopp Adler 867-190040-M (USD 4,895) [51] is the right head for future woven or heavy products and the only one with a vendor-documented PLC protocol (DAC ACCESS on DAC flex/comfort, not on the ECO's DAC eco) [54].

Decision 5: control architecture for heads. Selected: pattern 1 (OEM box driven by PLC I/O) for the first rig, with an EFKA AB321A or JUKI SC-922 box, pedal emulation by analog actuator or speed-command inputs, and 360/512-pulse rotation feedback into the PLC for stitch counting; migrate to pattern 3 (industrial servo on EtherCAT replacing the OEM motor, as in DLRoSS) only where the robot must stay in phase with the feed dog [57][62][120]. Risk: pattern 3 loses OEM trimmer and foot-lift sequencing; budget PLC code and air valves to re-create them.

Decision 6: automats and template machines. Do not buy a pattern sewer or template machine for the T-shirt: fixtures cannot stretch rib or follow a curling free edge [120]. ## Integration notes

- Mechanical: flat-bed heads (DDL-9000C, Jack W4, Brother S-7300A) mount in a table cut-out on rubber cushions per the manual drawing (JUKI DDL-9000C-F manual 2-1; Brother S-7250A 2-1) [42][48]; the cell should replace the wooden table with a machined aluminium plate using the same cut-out and ø17 mm drilled hole pattern. Overlock heads mount semi- or fully-sunken (JUKI MC-7A/MC-8/MC-9 mounting and auto-lifter options; Siruba TB-K1 supporter) [6][35]; a fully-sunken mount puts the throat plate flush with the robot's work surface, which the loader rollers need. Cylinder-bed coverstitch heads need free space under and around the arm for a tube (JUKI "front cover trimmed" and Yamato "extension cover for small parts") [84][22]. Reserve head masses of 27–46 kg (overlock/coverstitch) and 40–57 kg (lockstitch) plus 10–15 kg for the DD motor/box where integrated [1][81][40][49]. Vibration: isolate the sewing table from the vision gantry; JUKI rates 83 dB(A) at 6,500 sti/min for the MO-6800S [4].
- Electrical: boxes are single-phase 200–240 V (JUKI SC-922 also 100–120 V and 3-phase 200–240 V) at 330–520 VA per head [61][41][4]; JUKI AMS-221F wants 3-phase 200 V 400 VA [69]. PLC side: JUKI CN51 inputs are DC 5 V, −5 mA and outputs NPN open collector with +24 V/+5 V rails on the connector (JUKI connector HK034610220) [62]; EFKA ST2 outputs total 96 VA [58]; DAC ACCESS needs the machine-ID 9850 867030 and an RS232 adapter (9874 867020 for DAC flex; level converters 9850 806017 RS422 or 9850 911004 RS485 for DAC comfort), 115,200 bit/s [54]. Provide one isolated 24 V I/O block per head, a rotation-pulse input (360 or 512 pulses/rev) into a PLC high-speed counter, and equipotential bonding between head and box (EFKA requirement) [57].
- Pneumatic: Pegasus PP and PL devices, JUKI AK auto-lifters, Siruba UC anti-curl, Ho Hsing G60-2 pneumatic foot lift and the expansion-roller loaders are air driven; JUKI JTR needs 0.45–0.7 MPa at 23 L/min and AMS-221F 0.5–0.55 MPa at 1.8 dm³/min; Atlanta 996 needs 90 psi at 10 cfm [79][69][87]. Plan a 0.6 MPa dry-air header with per-station valve islands on the PLC fieldbus.
- Data: no vendor fieldbus on Japanese heads; the integration layer (M09) must publish head state from PLC I/O (running, needle up/down, trimmer done, foot up, thread-break from Eltex) and accept start/stop, speed command, foot lift, trimmer and chain-cut commands. For Dürkopp Adler heads M09 can wrap DAC ACCESS telegrams (GET_STATE, SET_VID stitch length / max speed, CMD_SIMULATE_PEDAL) and subscribe to PUSH values [54]. Keep stitch counting on the PLC from the pulse output; log stitches per seam and thread-break events to M14.
- Cables, connectors, mounts, drivers, software: JUKI CN51 mating connector HK034610220 with pins HK03464000A [62]; EFKA EB301A actuator, LSM002 light barrier, IPG001 encoder, IF232-USB interface and C200 compiler [57]; Ho Hsing SOP-6 standing pedal and external synchronisers 600-28/-29/-30 [66]; Eltex 15965 M12 cable [105]; DAC RemoteViewer for Windows to test DAC ACCESS before writing PLC code [53]. Software: no vendor SDK for JUKI/Pegasus/Yamato/Jack boxes; ROS-to-microcontroller shims are documented in [118]; for pattern 3, standard EtherCAT drives (Yaskawa SGD7S in [120]) under the cell's motion controller.
- Safety: the DD overlocks already block starting when the eye guard, cloth-plate cover or presser-foot arm sensors are open [3][8]; keep those sensors wired into the safety PLC rather than bypassed, add needle-area guarding for robot access, and use the box's rotation-inhibit input (JUKI "prohibit depressing front part of pedal", DAC X1.11 machine run blockage, EFKA mode 33) as the non-safety enable; the safety PLC must cut motor power independently (ST09).

## Conflicts with the white paper

1. The white paper calls reference [04] "the differential-feed research" (line 832). The paper (DLRoSS, arXiv 2607.29464) does not use the machine's differential feed to control the unequal lengths; it stretches the shorter ply with roller end-effectors on a robot while the overlock's feed is measured by a monitor roller. The white paper's conclusion ("supports studying an independent feed mechanism; does not validate automatic circular installation") stands, but the mechanism should be described as robot-side roller stretching, and the paper's overlock had its motor replaced by an EtherCAT servo [120].
2. The white paper cites JUKI's 2,400–3,000 bottom-hemming operations in nine hours (line 1336) correctly against the JTR manual [79]; the European dealer Impall quotes about 1,500 T-shirts per 8 h for the same machine [80]. Takt planning should use the lower dealer figure until measured.
3. The white paper's factory build scenarios (USD 52,000 / 83,000 / 140,000) assume the sewing functions are "separate permanently threaded modules" (lines 810–812). Three automation-grade heads cost about USD 5,000–11,000 complete at reseller prices (F12), but the collar module as specified (cut rib, join loop, load, distribute stretch) has no purchasable autonomous equivalent; operator-loaded workstations of that class are quote-only and estimated at USD 30,000–60,000 each (F12, est.). The lowest scenario is therefore unlikely to cover a bought-in collar workstation plus heads; it implies an in-house collar module built from a head, pneumatic expansion rollers and the ST04 manipulator.
4. The white paper treats Silana's "one worker per ten machines" and SoftWear's worklines as unaudited claims (lines 1708–1716); this research found no new public performance data for either, only SoftWear's August 2025 funding and its statement that the third-generation T-shirt workline was expected in H1 2026 [107][108]. No conflict, but no confirmation either.

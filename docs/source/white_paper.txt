Executive synthesis
The proposed system is an AI-directed roll-to-garment production cell: a user develops and approves a
garment in a conversational, visually grounded workspace, loads qualified fabric rolls and construction
materials, and receives complete cut-and-sewn garments. The base process includes material
preparation, cutting, component identification, robotic handling, sewing, required construction finishing,
inspection and discharge. Embroidery, printing and other processes can be added as complete,
integrated modules.
This is a product definition and engineering synthesis, not a description of an existing prototype. Its
central hypothesis is that a common garment representation, reusable construction skills and mechanics
designed for observable, recoverable handling can make a wide range of supported garments economical
in smaller batches. The intended scope includes T-shirts, blouses, sweatshirts, hoodies and other tops,
with longer garments added when their specific handling requirements are established.
The evidence supports taking that hypothesis seriously. Commercial equipment already addresses roll-
fed single-layer cutting and individual sewing operations; vendor demonstrations connect cutter
metadata to robotic component collection. Research demonstrates fixture-free sewing, controlled
feeding of unequal fabric lengths, panel transport, CAD-assisted alignment and multimodal generation of
parametric garment patterns. These are restricted capabilities with different starting conditions, not
proof of a complete production cell. [01–10]
The hardest integration work is maintaining control of a garment as it changes from separate flat pieces
into a connected three-dimensional object. A valid architecture must preserve or recover the intended
edges, isolate the correct layers, support unused fabric, maintain sewing access, avoid trapping fixtures
inside closed sections and release the finished item. AI can choose and correct actions only where the
hardware provides the necessary reach, support and sensing.
The system is not distinguished merely by using AI. CreateMe explicitly describes mechanics and
learned control developed together; Silana describes adaptive fabric handling and sewing; SoftWear
promotes machine-vision garment worklines; and Resonance connects design intent to production
orchestration. The proposed distinction is a combination to be demonstrated: conventional stitched
construction, roll-to-output continuity, reusable capabilities across garment families, inexpensive job
changes and a deployable product for smaller manufacturers. [14–19]

What the economics show—and do not show
Two profit models are kept separate. For the machine supplier, illustrative build-and-test costs of
$52,000, $83,000 and $140,000 produce very different results at a $100,000 selling price. After
assumed delivery, warranty and variable selling costs, contribution is respectively $33,000, $2,000 and
negative $55,000 per system. None of those build costs is a supplier quotation. The comparison shows
how strongly the price objective depends on physical simplification and repeatable manufacturing.
For the garment operator, a worked premium blank T-shirt model assumes $6.08 in variable cost per
acceptable garment and $293.86 in daily fixed operating cost including depreciation. At 100 acceptable
garments sold per day for an assumed $12 ex-factory price, modeled operating profit is about $298 per
day, or $74,415 across 250 operating days. At 50 sales per day the same model is approximately break-
even. These are conditional arithmetic examples, not a margin forecast, market-price claim or guarantee
of demand. Sections 20–22 expose every material assumption and the effects of lower utilization, higher
labor and worse yield.

  The project’s commercial test is not simply whether a robot can sew a shirt. It is whether one
  repeatable system can deliver approved quality, useful variety and competitive total cost across real
  small orders, without hiding labor or preparation outside the measured process.

01 / Purpose, scope and evidence
A research compilation, not a product certification
This white paper establishes a common technical and commercial definition for the proposed system. It
brings the mechanics, software, garment requirements, production model, competitive landscape and
unit economics into one document. All monetary amounts are in United States dollars unless otherwise
stated; prices and rates in the numerical models are explicit assumptions rather than quoted market
offers.

shipping functionality. Research abstracts and author pages demonstrate only the tasks they report.
Where a source starts with already aligned pieces, a person loading a hemmer or chemically stiffened
fabric, those conditions are retained in the analysis rather than silently removed.

Schematics in this document are original functional diagrams. They are not dimensioned fabrication

Terminology that prevents misleading comparisons
A complete garment has all construction operations in its approved specification finished. An acceptable
garment also meets the agreed inspection criteria. A production attempt can end in acceptance,
rejection or an interrupted cycle. A recipe is a versioned, qualified combination of design limits, material
profile, tools, construction sequence and quality checks.
Autonomous assembly means no person manipulates the garment between the defined operations.
Unattended batch operation additionally requires sufficient supplies, waste and output capacity, and
acceptable handling of faults without intervention. An automated changeover is a separate capability: a
machine can assemble a batch autonomously yet still require an operator to change rolls or thread
before the next batch.
A completion interval is the time between outputs of a running pipeline. Lead time is the elapsed time
for one garment to pass through the cell. Accepted output is not equivalent to needle cycles, completed
hems or nominal robot speed. Contribution is revenue less specified variable costs; it is not company net
profit. These distinctions are applied consistently to the performance and economic scenarios.

Scope of the conclusion
The synthesis identifies a credible engineering route based on existing process technology and
demonstrated manipulation capabilities. It does not establish unrestricted garment support, a $100,000
delivered system, a particular footprint, specified shift output or commercially sufficient reliability. The
development program must establish those properties together. A negative result on one architecture is
evidence to redesign that architecture, not proof that intelligent garment manufacturing as a category is
physically impossible.



02 / The product: one controlled material-to-
garment process
The proposed product is a coordinated industrial system rather than a collection of independently
operated machines. A single application manages the approved garment, production recipe, material
inventory, cutting layout, component identity, station scheduling, inspection results and output record.
Different modules can work on different garments simultaneously, but their handoffs share a defined
control contract.




Figure 1. Base-system scope. The proposed unattended run excludes hidden manual construction; setup and retail handling
remain explicitly visible.


What is inside the base process
The base system accepts qualified conventional textiles and construction materials, performs the
necessary preparation, cuts the actual pattern components, extracts them without losing identity,
assembles them and completes the required finishing. It must handle scrap, defective pieces and
incomplete jobs rather than depend on an operator to sort them during a successful run.
The user can be required to load rolls into specified positions, thread or fit approved supply cartridges
before Start, confirm material identity and approve a production job. A deliberate setup process is
compatible with autonomous production. Conversely, repeated human placement of a neckline, manual
fabric turning or a final hand-sewn closure is not autonomous assembly simply because the needle
movement is automated.

What is outside the baseline
Fabric manufacture, yarn production, dyeing, special garment washes and broad chemical finishing are
outside the baseline. Retail folding, packaging and shipping can be external or optional. A garment
needing pressing or fusing to complete its actual construction cannot be advertised as complete while
that step is excluded. Decoration modules have their own process requirements, utility limits and
throughput ratings.
The term “any fabric” is not used. A released configuration must identify the material formats and
properties it supports. Open-width jersey, woven fabric and ribbing are different handling cases; tubular
ribbing, very slippery cloth, coated textiles or heavy seam stacks require explicit qualification. A target
garment family is an intended capability, not evidence that every material and detail within that family is
supported.

Operator experience
The intended journey is: create a project; upload references and specifications; select a qualified
material; refine the garment; approve the technical record; load or verify supplies; authorize the batch;
and retrieve accepted garments with a production record. The system should present a reasoned
alternative when a request exceeds its capabilities before fabric is cut. It should never quietly simplify a
neckline, replace a stitch with adhesive or omit a detail to make the physical task easier.

The integration problem is state continuity
Each module must receive something it can actually work with. A cutting system may know a piece’s
location at the instant of cutting, but extraction changes its shape. A sewing head may accept two
aligned layers, but the previous operation may deliver a partially closed garment with the relevant edge
underneath another panel. A hemmer may sew a tube reliably after loading, yet loading that tube is itself
a manipulation task.
The proposed platform therefore treats each transition as a first-class operation. It must know which
object is present, where the next edge should be, which supports currently control it, which layers
belong under the needle and whether the receiving station is ready. A motion command is not sufficient
evidence that the intended transfer happened.

Why an industrial cell is a better initial physical format
Dedicated work zones allow permanently threaded sewing functions, accessible fixtures, material
buffers and serviceable modules. A single-purpose industrial head can be retained while its loading and
unloading are automated. This avoids requiring every process to be miniaturized and combined in one
compact enclosure at the same time that the handling method is being developed.
That is an architectural advantage, not a claim that the proposed price is easy. More stations can
increase cost, floor area, calibration effort and shared-transport demand. A larger cell still needs a

04 / Physical architecture and module interfaces
The starting architecture is a hybrid modular cell. Predictable operations use simple supports and
fixtures where those reduce complexity; variable operations use active grippers and sensor feedback.
Neither “no fixtures” nor “maximum robots” is a requirement. The objective is controlled fabric access at
acceptable cost, with as little garment-specific rebuilding as practicable.




Figure 2. Relational layout of the proposed cell. Module positions convey workflow, not dimensions or a verified robot reach
envelope.


Functional zones
The front end contains roll loading, controlled feed, inspection, conditioning where required and a
supported cutting area. A registered buffer holds identified parts and growing garments. Construction
modules provide structural joining, rib and neckline operations, hems, closures and required forming or
fusing. An output zone separates accepted garments, rejects and recoverable work. Utilities include
controls, guarding, air or vacuum generation or declared plant connections, lint extraction, waste
collection and maintenance access.
A gantry can provide repeatable planar coverage while an articulated manipulator offers lifting and
reorientation. Opening carriers can retain panel identity and support selected regions. The appropriate
combination depends on the largest parts, seam access, material response, payload with tooling, transfer
time and the ability to release a closed garment. Selection should follow real-fabric trials and kinematic
analysis, not the visual appeal of a robot arm.

The receiving module acknowledges control before the source releases fabric. The controller records a
transition from one verified state to the next, not merely “robot move completed.” A failed
acknowledgment should not leave a garment unsupported between machines.

Space, mass and utilities
No exact footprint or weight is assigned at this stage. A layout must include roll diameter and mass,
maximum fabric width, cutting travel, panel swept volumes, tool reach, output accumulation, guarding
and maintenance clearance. Holding a panel does not establish that the joined garment can rotate or
escape the same workspace. Additional floor area may be required for loaded rolls and safe loading
equipment even if they are outside the guarded moving zone.
Utilities must be measured for the complete system. Sewing motors, extraction, compressed air, vacuum
hold-down and compute draw power differently; printing or fusing can add heat and ventilation
requirements. Shared plant air is not “free” simply because it is absent from the machine cabinet. The
supplier specification should identify peak demand, average duty, heat rejection, air quality and service
isolation requirements.

Buy, adapt or build
Established process modules can accelerate a prototype, while purpose-designed mechanisms may be
necessary for a viable repeat-build cost. Each outsourced module needs an accessible control interface,
fault reporting, maintenance documentation and a defined support boundary. Research availability is not
permission to reproduce patented hardware or commercially redistribute code. Supplier integration
rights and licensing require separate review; no clearance determination is made here.



05 / Materials, roll handling and dimensional
stability
“Load a roll” is an input format, not proof that the material is ready for accurate manufacture. Oshima’s
preparation documentation identifies retained tension from production, finishing, storage and transport
as a reason to relax fabric before cutting. The necessary method and duration depend on the material. A
fast in-cell feeder cannot be assumed to replace all conditioning. [11]

A qualified material profile
The material record should include composition where known, construction, mass per area, thickness,
usable width, lot, face and grain directions, stretch under a defined test load, recovery, surface friction,
edge curl, porosity, shrinkage after the agreed wash protocol and sensitivity to pressure or heat. These
are proposed qualification fields, not values inferred confidently from a photograph or fabric trade name.
For each profile the system needs acceptable ranges and handling settings. “Cotton jersey, 280 gsm” is
not a complete robotics specification. Two materials carrying that description can require different grip
pressure, tension, blade settings or seam feed. A new supplier lot may be accepted through a defined
verification procedure or require a new profile rather than silent reuse of the old one.

Controlled material delivery
A candidate feed path uses a roll cradle, controlled unwind, tension measurement, edge guidance and an
accumulation section before the cutting surface. The system must detect end-of-roll, skew, folds,
unusable width and relevant visible defects. A supported material may require a longer preparation
buffer or an explicitly stabilized incoming roll. The stated production time must include whatever
preparation the cell actually performs.
The cutter should receive stable fabric without pulling it into an incorrect shape. Low tension is not the
same as uncontrolled slack: excessive slack can introduce wrinkles and placement errors. Closed-loop
feed should keep the material inside its qualified handling envelope. The control policy must be tested
when roll diameter changes and when a fresh roll is loaded, not only at one convenient state.

Multiple materials and colors
A shirt may use body fabric, rib, tape and labels from different supplies. A woven blouse may add
interfacing and closures. The system therefore needs separate input identities and synchronization. A
production job reserves all required components before irreversible work begins, reducing the chance of
cutting a body that cannot be completed because its matching rib is unavailable.
Automatic switching between rolls, thread colors and trim cartridges is an additional mechanical
capability. A basic configuration can require disclosed setup before a compatible batch while still being
autonomous during that batch. The software should expose those restrictions when estimating a mixed-
order schedule. A user selecting a new color on screen does not establish that a roll and every matching
thread path have changed themselves.

Consumption and batch capacity
The model must count input material used for nested shapes, cut waste, failed parts, quality rejects,
setup pieces and rework. Remaining roll length should be tracked with uncertainty and checked against
the next batch. A system intended to operate unattended also needs sufficient rib, thread, tape, label and
output capacity. End-of-supply detection is necessary but does not by itself provide automatic
replenishment.
Section 21 gives a mass-balanced example. Under its assumptions, 100 acceptable heavyweight T-shirts
consume approximately 43.2 kg of body and rib fabric including layout waste and rejects. That is a model
result, not a general material requirement; actual pattern areas, weights and yields determine the
number. Roll logistics must be sized to those actual requirements rather than the headline count of
garments.



06 / Cutting, nesting, extraction and scrap
The proposed starting process is supported single-layer knife cutting, chosen for study because it can
place different sizes and approved designs in a shared layout. Multi-ply cutting may be attractive for
larger repeated orders, but it introduces stack registration and singulation requirements. The
architecture should compare both against the intended order mix rather than declare either universally
superior.
Lectra advertises automated roll-out, camera-assisted positioning and a 120 m/min cutting-path speed
for Virga Fashion. That number is neither garment throughput nor fabric-roll consumption. Notches,
curves, acceleration, feed, material support and offloading all contribute to cycle time. RobotFactory’s
textile application demonstrates the distinct next step: using cutter metadata to collect and sort
identified T-shirt pieces before they go to sewing. [01,02]

Nesting is constrained optimization
A marker must respect grain direction, stretch direction, face, nap, print placement, pattern matching,
usable width, known defects and seam allowances. A layout with high area utilization is not acceptable if
it rotates a sleeve against the approved grain. A cutting optimizer also needs to consider downstream
demand: cutting many panels for blocked stations can create excessive work in process and bury
components required for urgent jobs.
Each cut component receives a digital identity linked to its garment and material lot. That identity need
not require a permanent visible mark. Known cutting location, temporary machine-readable labels where
approved, controlled carriers and verified transfer can maintain identity. Physical labeling decisions must
consider garment appearance, adhesive residue, washing and any later cutting or sewing operation.

Extraction is not just picking something up
After cutting, the system must determine whether the perimeter is complete, separate the panel from
surrounding waste and lift it without excessive distortion. Small notches, bridges, curled edges and
adjacent pieces can complicate pickup. A panel-shaped vacuum pattern may help on some materials but
leak or lift unwanted layers on others. A pinch or hybrid gripper requires safe contact regions and
reliable edge access.
Scrap removal is coordinated with pickup. Pulling away the unused web must not drag a completed
piece into the waste stream or move remaining components beyond recognition. The production
controller should track extraction outcomes and distinguish an absent panel from a panel that was
picked and misidentified. A recut must use a new part identity tied to the same garment need, so the
rejected original cannot accidentally re-enter assembly.

What the cutter hands to assembly
The key output is not a bin of fabric. It is a verified component in a usable supported state, with identity,
orientation, actual geometry, known defects and a clear next operation. This is a proposed extension
beyond cutter-linked sorting. It reduces ambiguity at the assembly entry point while acknowledging that
geometry changes whenever cloth is lifted, folded or released.
A buffer should store components in a way that preserves access and avoids flattening one garment
under many unrelated kits. There may be separate buffers for flat pieces and sewn subassemblies. Their
sizes follow the timing model and fault behavior, not an arbitrary number of trays. A full buffer must
cause controlled upstream slowing or replanning rather than physical overflow.

Cutting-related validation
Acceptance tests should cover largest and smallest parts, narrow rib strips, notches, local curves, roll
skew, empty areas, varying porosity, visible defects and incomplete-cut recovery. Record geometric
error after release, extraction success, material damage, lost identity, recut workload and scrap fraction.
A cutter capable of accurate flat patterns is useful evidence; autonomous transfer in the condition
needed for sewing is the additional system milestone.



07 / Manipulation: controlling cloth rather than
commanding coordinates
The proposed controller operates on the observed cloth state. It uses the known garment and
completed-seam record to narrow the task, then cameras and contact feedback to establish where the
material actually is. A fixed coordinate can be a starting estimate; it is not a substitute for checking that
the intended edge is present and the correct layers are controlled.

This is a comparison agenda, not a selected bill of materials. RollUP provides research precedent for

Recovering the next edge
A folded edge is not automatically a terminal failure. A qualified skill can observe, lift a known region,
expose an edge, grasp it and verify the result. But the mechanism needs the necessary degrees of
freedom, free space and support. If a carrier blocks access or a garment is trapped around a solid
support, the solution requires a different fixture or assembly order, not merely a more confident model
prediction.
The controller should separate uncertainty about identity from uncertainty about pose. It may know
which panel is present but not exactly where its edge lies. It may instead see a plausible edge but be
unable to confirm whether it is a hem, a folded layer or a matching seam. Those conditions warrant
different probes and stop thresholds. Force or tactile checks should be used where they add
discrimination rather than treated as decorative sensors.

Entry conditions and bounded skills
A reusable skill has a required starting state, tools, observations, adjustable parameters, success test and
permitted recovery path. For example, “align and sew” requires known mating edges, controlled layer
count, an accessible lead-in, adequate thread and support for unused cloth. It must not begin simply
because the next line in a program says “sew.”
A skill library can cover exposure, pickup, flattening, tensioning, alignment, feeding, folding, turning,
transfer and release. Generality comes from parameters and observed state, but only within an
evaluated envelope. New garment sizes and materials should be tested against those limits. A novel
combination of familiar skills can still fail because one operation leaves the next without access or
adequate support.

Learning and physical data
Training records should include unsuccessful grasps, doubles, slip, occlusion, malformed seams and
recovery attempts, not only ideal runs. Physical data must connect sensor observations to action,
material, geometry and outcome. Simulation can help explore reach, scheduling and candidate policies,
but real textile response and seam quality require physical testing. A learned policy is released as a
versioned component; improvements are evaluated offline before replacing a qualified production
version.

08 / Stitching, ribbing, closures and construction
tools
The base system preserves conventional cut-and-sew construction. Its capability derives from the
combination of stitch-forming mechanisms and autonomous material presentation. A sewing head’s
ability to create a stitch does not establish that the robot can introduce the correct layers, follow the
seam, avoid neighboring cloth, finish the thread path and remove the garment.

A proposed process toolbox
Structural knit seams call for appropriate overlock capability; visible knit hems call for coverstitch
capability; many woven assemblies and topstitch details call for lockstitch capability. Those functions
can be implemented as separate permanently threaded modules in the initial industrial architecture.
Buttonholes, button attachment, bartacks, selected zipper operations and fusing require their own
qualified tools or modules where included in a released garment configuration.
Automatic needle or thread conversion is not presumed. Multiple heads may simplify process changes
but add space, thread paths, maintenance and transport. Automatic thread trimming and break detection
do not eliminate all thread faults. Any necessary bobbin, looper-thread, needle or tool intervention is
either automated and validated, completed before the run or included as an interruption.




Figure 5. Split-support ribbed-neckline concept. A possible support-and-release arrangement; its geometry and full loop
handling are unproven.


The ribbed collar as an integrated benchmark
A complete collar module must cut the strip from the designated rib supply, join its ends where required,
establish face and orientation, form the fold, present it to the neckline, distribute local stretch, sew the
approved seam, perform tape or topstitch steps and release the garment. It cannot receive a manually
prepared loop while being described as roll-to-garment automation.
The differential-feed research demonstrates controlled sewing of unequal-length stretchable layers, but
its prepared leading ends and straight seam are narrower than this complete task. It supports studying an
independent feed mechanism; it does not validate automatic circular installation. [04]
For a numerical illustration, a 50 cm body neckline with a 42.5 cm relaxed rib seam line has a nominal
ratio of 0.85. That is not a universal rib rule. Rib recovery, stretch force, desired appearance, seam
allowance and the actual material pair determine the approved relationship. The system must control
local distribution rather than simply stretch the entire loop to a target length once.

A proposed opening support would retract or split after sewing. It must leave the rest of the shirt
supported while the neckline is unloaded. Cameras and contact checks should distinguish a correctly
placed loop from a twist or an unwanted captured layer. A collar looking symmetrical under tension is
not enough; geometry, recovery and seam appearance should be checked after release and during wash
qualification.

Turning, fusing and closures
A woven collar or cuff may require shaped joining, trimming, turning, pressing and topstitching. Those
are different manipulations from feeding a flat seam. Facings and interfacing introduce additional layers
and material compatibility. A module must provide a controlled way to apply and release heat or
pressure without damaging the garment or creating a fire or entanglement hazard.
Closures require defined supply formats and presentation. A button must be oriented, placed and
attached; its corresponding buttonhole must exist and be verified. A zipper requires accurate tape
placement, allowance management and the specified end treatment. A drawcord operation involves
feeding, locating ends and verifying the result. These operations should appear in the recipe and costing
rather than disappear into a generic “attach trims” label.

Finishing equivalence matters
An open-flat hem before side closure can differ at the seam intersection from a specified tubular hem
completed afterward. A simpler neckline construction can differ from a folded rib collar. The compiler
may propose a more machine-friendly method, but the customer must approve any resulting
construction change. Software optimization is constrained by the actual garment specification, not
merely by an approximate silhouette.

10 / The conversational design and production
workspace
The user interface is the primary entry point, but it is not the entire intelligence of the system. Its
purpose is to turn intent into a consistent approved garment, expose manufacturing constraints early
and preserve the connection between design changes and physical production. It should be chat-led and
visually grounded rather than a text-only sequence of prompts.




Figure 8. Proposed application workspace: context, conversational revision, engineering tools and production status linked to
one approved record.


Persistent projects and reference handling
A project can contain photographs, sketches, pattern files, measurement tables, fabric documentation,
prior technical packs, sample feedback and production results. The reference archive is searchable and
persistent; the currently approved garment is a separate structured record. Older images should not
silently override an explicit later decision. Each extracted fact should retain its source and confidence
where uncertainty affects manufacture.
A photograph can suggest a silhouette or detail but does not establish an exact dimension, fabric
composition or tested stretch profile. The application must request missing measurements or label
estimates before approval. Extensive stored context does not imply unlimited active model context or
perfect memory. Relevant retrieval, structured decisions and version history are the proposed means of
managing large projects reliably.

Interactive design functions
The workspace should display the garment, pattern geometry and technical specification together. A
user might select a sleeve and request a two-centimeter length change, compare necklines, specify a rib
width or choose between approved construction alternatives. The change should update every
dependent output, not only a rendering. GarmentCode and Design2GarmentCode provide evidence for
parameterized pattern construction and multimodal pattern-program generation; the physical
production checks remain an additional layer. [08,09]
The interface distinguishes a concept preview, an engineering-checked design, a physically qualified
recipe and an approved production job. These are different states. A visually convincing garment can
have mismatched seam lengths or require a tool the cell lacks. A physically qualified base recipe can
become unqualified after a significant material or construction change.

Technical documentation generated from the record
Proposed outputs include a versioned technical pack, measurement and tolerance table, panel list,
material and trim specification, grain/face annotations, seam map, stitch requirements, cutting marker,
construction sequence, labeling instructions, machine configuration and inspection plan. Documents are
generated from the same source data used by the execution system. A rendering or narrative description
is not a substitute for actual cutting geometry.
Before production, the app should show estimated material consumption, required input rolls,
machine/tool compatibility, expected setup, order quantity and the assumptions underlying time and
cost. A visible approval action authorizes a fixed revision. Later changes create a new revision and
cannot silently modify jobs already in progress.

Production and exception experience
The user should see jobs waiting, cutting, assembled, inspected, accepted or rejected, with reason codes
for interruptions. Operators need actionable instructions for authorized setup and service, not opaque
model output. A remote person guiding a robot through a failure is still an intervention and must be
recorded as such.
Where multiple customers share a production cell, access controls separate their design files,
measurements and outputs. Reference permissions, retention choices and deletion requirements should
be explicit. A customer’s private design must not automatically become training data for other users.
Production telemetry can be useful without retaining every identifiable reference or body-measurement
file indefinitely.

A seam graph connects specific panel edges rather than assuming that two nearby visible boundaries
should be sewn. Edge correspondence can include notches and local easing constraints. Different seam
lengths are not automatically errors: intentional rib stretch or easing must be represented explicitly.
Unintentional mismatch must not be “fixed” by the robot stretching a layer until it fits.

Compilation and validation stages
The compiler first checks geometry, complete panel definitions, allowances and edge correspondences.
It then checks material and tool compatibility, generates a candidate assembly dependency graph and
evaluates whether each step can be supported, reached and released. Station allocation and cutting
preparation follow only when the recipe can be executed on the actual cell configuration.
Physical access is the crucial extra check beyond pattern validity. A proposed seam order may close a
tube before a required label or cuff is installed, trap a carrier, hide an edge from every gripper or require
a turn the mechanism cannot perform. The compiler should search permitted alternative sequences and
explain unsupported requests. It cannot certify real-cloth behavior solely from geometric simulation.

Change propagation
A neckline edit changes the panel edge, rib geometry, length relationship, cutting layout and
construction parameters. A material substitution may affect shrinkage compensation, gripping, feed,
pressing and quality qualification even when the silhouette is unchanged. A closure change can alter
both the part count and assembly order. The system should invalidate dependent outputs until the
relevant checks pass again.
Software-only variation is a desired outcome, not an assumption. Within an approved parameter range, a
new size might reuse tools and skills. Outside that range, the machine may need a different support or
qualification. The application must make that boundary visible to the user before an order is promised.

Executable jobs and module contracts
A job package should include signed or otherwise integrity-protected recipe and policy versions, material
identities, station requirements, entry conditions, allowed parameter ranges, recovery limits, quality
checks and a log schema. Each module accepts only instructions compatible with its capabilities and
current state. Type-checked interfaces reduce ambiguity but do not replace physical validation.
The compiler and scheduler can use search, rules, optimization and learned models where appropriate.
The most valuable output is a verifiable plan, not the presence of an LLM in every software layer. A
repeat order should reuse an approved recipe while adapting to measured cloth conditions rather than
re-invent its construction from free-form text.



12 / AI, robotics and deterministic control
The system is designed around adaptive physical execution, not a chatbot directly driving motors.
Learned models can interpret references, estimate cloth state, select manipulation skills and predict
outcomes. Deterministic software can enforce geometry, interfaces and scheduling constraints. Local
controllers execute synchronized motion, while independent safety functions can inhibit it regardless of
model output.




Figure 10. Proposed control stack. The safety path is independent of conversational inference and cloud connectivity.


What “designed for AI” should mean
The hardware should make relevant material states observable and recoverable: cameras need useful
sight lines; grippers need ways to expose edges; supports need controlled release; and tools need
measurable entry conditions. Mechanical simplification and sensing can reduce the uncertainty that a
learned policy must handle. The goal is not to remove all structure and demand that a general model
solve an arbitrary pile of cloth.
This principle is not unique to the project. The competitive comparison later describes other approaches
that explicitly combine hardware and intelligence. The proposed engineering advantage must be
demonstrated in reduced setup, broader supported variation, fewer interventions or better cost, not
inferred from the phrase “AI-native.”

Skill execution and recovery




Figure 11. Proposed skill-level control. Retries are bounded and unsuccessful attempts remain visible in the production record.

A skill checks its preconditions, executes an action while observing material state and tests the outcome.
Recoverable deviations can lead to re-observation, a controlled regrip, recut or rerouting. An uncertain
or dangerous state leads to a safe stop. The retry budget should consider fabric damage and elapsed
time; unlimited attempts can destroy both quality and shift output.
Research on phase-conditioned imitation learning reports autonomous recovery in T-shirt hanging and
removal. This is useful evidence that recovery can be part of a learned controller, but it is not a
demonstration of repairing sewn defects or operating this cell. The manufacturing skill set requires its
own data, checks and validation. [29]

Training, simulation and deployment
A practical development process collects demonstrations and failures on representative fabrics, parts
and intermediate assemblies. Simulation supports geometric checks, policy exploration and scheduling,
with a measured gap to real materials. A test set should include withheld parameter combinations,
changes in illumination, material lots, edge curl and plausible misalignment. Good performance on
repeated training pieces does not establish generalization.
Policies and models remain versioned. Production machines should not autonomously rewrite their
qualified behavior after each garment. New versions are tested offline, compared with baselines,
released deliberately and reversible. Controlled remote updates, rollback and a local ability to stop safely
are product requirements. Model confidence must be calibrated against actual error, especially where a
false-positive quality check could pass a defective garment.

Latency and failure independence
Critical motion and sewing synchronization remain local. Network loss should not create uncontrolled
movement or leave the plant unable to reach a safe state. A local execution package can continue an
already approved job when that behavior is explicitly designed and qualified; otherwise it pauses safely.
Cloud services can provide design inference, retrieval, fleet analytics and planning without being the
only path to safety.
The general AI risk-management process should document foreseeable failures, data limitations,
monitoring and release controls. NIST’s AI Risk Management Framework is a useful voluntary
governance reference, not machinery certification or proof of safe robot behavior. [26]

13 / Scheduling, capacity and scalable output
A production cell can work on several garments at once. One item may be receiving a collar while
another is cut and another is hemmed. This separates the elapsed time for an individual garment from
the interval between completed outputs. The proposed architecture should exploit that overlap without
exceeding shared transport or buffer capacity.




Figure 12. Illustrative pipeline. A–D are different garments; windows are not timed claims. A real model must reserve every
station and shared handling resource.


Output arithmetic
For a simplified steady-state model, let H be available shift minutes, A the fraction effectively available
for production, Y the acceptable yield and c the running interval between completed attempts. Then
acceptable output is approximately G = H x A x Y / c. The factors must be defined so a delay or reject is
not deducted twice. If c already measures the all-in achieved interval, additional availability or
performance deductions may be inappropriate.

Cold starts, buffers and unfinished work
A full-shift test begins from a declared state: loaded rolls, conditioned or unconditioned inputs, threaded
tools, empty or pre-filled buffers and installed software versions. Required in-cell preparation, pipeline

the cell is already full is not the same as a cold-start roll-to-output count.
Buffers absorb variation but consume space and material. In a stable hypothetical pipeline completing an
attempt every two minutes with a 20-minute manufacturing lead time, average work in process is
approximately ten garments. That relationship is a planning example; actual routing, rejects and multiple
visits can require different capacities. An additional buffer does not increase capacity when a single
resource remains fully occupied.

Embroidery
A suitable panel can be embroidered before it becomes difficult to access in a closed garment. The
complete process includes design digitization, stitch and density checks, correct backing, automatic
clamping or hooping, thread management, stitching, trim operations, backing finishing where necessary
and release. The location must be registered to the actual panel and sewing allowances.
Commercial multi-needle embroidery equipment provides a process precedent; Tajima’s SAI, for
example, has eight needles and specified frames. That does not supply automatic panel loading,
stabilization or downstream garment transfer. Those remain integration work, and machine compatibility
with thicker stacks or particular backing materials must be tested. [12]
The design must not create an unhandled manual backing-removal step. Thread breaks, color changes,
needles and empty supplies also matter to unattended operation. Dense embroidery may dominate cycle
time; adding a second head helps only when loading, power, frames and transport can support
concurrent work. The base shirt’s output rating cannot be applied to arbitrary embroidery.

Direct print, transfers and roll print
Candidate routes include printing a flat panel before sewing, printing qualified roll material before
cutting, or using a finished-garment station where loading is feasible. The chosen route depends on
artwork, substrate, registration, heat exposure and the desired finish. Ordinary cotton garments cannot
simply be assumed compatible with every print chemistry.
Brother’s GTX300 documentation describes pretreatment and drying requirements, including
pretreatment for white-ink direct-to-garment work. Therefore a proposed print module must include all
required treatment, printing, fixing, cooling and inspection for the selected process, not only a printhead.
Specific temperature and dwell settings are recipe-dependent and must be validated on the actual
material and equipment. [13]
Transfer-based processes add adhesive or powder handling, placement and pressing requirements
where used. Roll printing can change the material’s dimensions or surface before cutting, requiring
subsequent registration and conditioning. Hot, wet or uncured panels must not be sent directly into
grippers or sewing stations without a qualified handling state.

Other functions and process ownership
Decorative stitching, selected heat transfers, special closures, retail pressing, folding and packaging are
possible modules to study. A garment requiring a closure or interfacing to complete construction must
include that operation in its advertised production configuration even if the machinery is physically
modular. “Optional” should mean optional to the chosen product, not missing from its promised output.
Each add-on specification declares input state, material range, consumables, heat or air requirements,
exhaust, cycle-time distribution, inspection, fault paths and release state. A bypass should maintain base
operation when a decoration module is absent or unavailable, provided the current job does not require
it. Routing a decorated job around a failed module and calling it complete would be a specification
failure.



15 / Quality, reliability and honest autonomy
Quality is defined against an approved sample and technical record. A garment that resembles the
intended shape can still fail on seam placement, collar recovery, puckering, skipped stitches, trim
location or dimensions. Online inspection and offline qualification have different functions; neither can
be replaced by a persuasive image.




Figure 14. Three independent measurement systems: machinery performance, garment quality and customer economics.


Inspection and qualification
The proposed inline checks include identity and lot verification, cut geometry, layer count, seam path,
thread presence, visible defects, collar/cuff symmetry, labels and completed construction. Confidence
thresholds and known blind spots are documented. Some defects require tactile or force observations;

A machine may remain unattended by rejecting a problem garment and continuing, but accepted yield

Why small failure probabilities accumulate
Under an illustrative independent-trial model, if each garment has a 1% chance of requiring a person, the
probability that 100 garments finish without such an intervention is approximately 36.6%. Across 500
garments it falls to 0.66%. At a 0.1% per-garment intervention probability, those batch probabilities are
approximately 90.5% and 60.6% respectively.
To achieve a 95% chance of an intervention-free 500-garment batch under that simplified model, the
per-garment probability must be about 0.0103% or lower. Actual faults can cluster by material lot, tool
wear or environment, so this is not a reliability prediction. It shows why “usually works” is insufficient for
an unattended-shift claim.

fabric lots, setup, total work time, recovery, operator minutes, rejects and accepted garments. Results on
repeated identical cloth should be separated from mixed lots and recipes. A short highlight video or a
sequence with failures removed is not a capacity or reliability dataset.

Situations where the system may be a poor fit

making plain knit tops cannot be compared with the price of fully decorated, washed and packed retail
products. Similarly, a cheaper outsourced quote may omit shipping, duties, inspection, sampling or

production always wins.

The setup-cost mechanism
As a simplified illustration, $200 of style-preparation cost adds $10 per item to a 20-piece batch, but
only $1 to a 200-piece batch. Reducing that preparation to $20 reduces those additions to $1 and $0.10
respectively. The value comes from lowering setup, not merely distributing the same setup over more
units. These values are not industry benchmarks.
The cell’s scheduling rules should therefore aim to minimize the expensive changes that actually exist:
rolls, thread colors, tools, cure conditions or unqualified construction details. Jobs can be grouped when
compatible without changing the customer’s approved garment.

Inventory and working stock
Smaller finished-garment runs can shift some stock from style-specific finished goods toward reusable
fabric and trims. That does not eliminate inventory risk; the operator or supplier now carries more
shared material and can still face obsolete colors, excess widths or unused specialty trims. The economic
test includes remaining roll value, unused cut pieces, minimum purchasing quantities and carrying costs
where material.
Smaller replenishment may reduce unsold finished stock or cash tied to large orders. Measure those
customer-specific effects from actual demand; do not add assumed retail margin to the manufacturing
benefit.

Labor, skills and production geography
The proposed system changes the allocation of work rather than establishing a labor-free factory. Setup,
garment engineering, material qualification, quality review, maintenance, logistics and customer
operations remain. The operator model in this paper deliberately includes paid work. Whether
automated assembly reduces total labor cost depends on how much service and exception handling it
introduces.
Local or regional production becomes attractive only when total economics support it. Energy, rent,
service, material supply, utilization and logistics still matter. A modular cell may enable a different
production footprint, but it does not guarantee lower landed cost in every country or displace all large-
scale conventional manufacturing.

Environmental effects require measurement
Potential benefits include lower finished-goods overproduction, fewer unnecessary transfers and better
traceability. Potential costs include energy for robotics, vacuum, air and heat, electronic equipment,
reject fabric, additional chemistry and more frequent small shipments. Easier production can also
stimulate more consumption. No percentage reduction in emissions or waste is assumed.
An evaluation should compare the full lifecycle and product durability, not only factory electricity or one
shipping leg. UNEP’s textile value-chain roadmap treats sustainability as a chain-wide issue; automation
is one possible contributor rather than a complete solution. Measure material utilization, rejected mass,
energy per acceptable garment, consumables, shipment patterns, repairability and garment life under a
consistent boundary. [27]

supplier’s delivery allowance and the operator’s site work are different items. The model excludes a
financing arrangement and any location-specific tax or duty treatment.
Six paid hours do not mean a person sews for six hours inside an autonomous run. They recognize that
loading, inspection, packaging, order management and service availability still require paid effort. The
exact staffing arrangement must provide safe access and adequate response; higher labor is tested in
Section 22. The 5 kW average is an energy assumption, not a measured system specification, and
excludes thermal decoration modules.

Real changes can be correlated. A more difficult fabric can reduce marker utilization, slow handling and
increase rejects together. A higher output configuration can require more labor and electricity.
Combining only favorable independent sensitivities would overstate profit. A mature model should use
measured cycle-time and defect distributions by material and recipe.

Decoration and branded-product economics
Decoration contributes a separate selling price and separate costs: ink or thread, backing, pretreatment,
transfer media, heat, dwell, module depreciation, rejects and handling. A $5 decoration surcharge is not
$5 of profit. If the decoration station constrains output, it also changes the fixed-cost allocation of the
entire cell. A higher-value garment can justify slower production, but only when buyers actually pay the
corresponding price.
A brand selling a garment at retail must still pay for customer acquisition, transactions, fulfillment,
returns, customer service, design and inventory. The operator’s modeled $12 ex-factory revenue is not
automatically the brand’s manufacturing cost in every arrangement, and the brand’s retail price cannot
be used as a proxy for factory savings. Profit should be attributed to the business that performs the
work and carries the risk.

Benchmarking the alternative
The comparison with outsourced production should match fabric, construction, quantity, quality, lead
time, decoration, packaging and delivery terms. The comparison with an internal conventional team
should include its actual productivity, overhead and flexibility. New-machine benefits may include lower
minimums, faster repeat orders, traceability or less finished inventory, but these should be quantified
separately rather than used to excuse a weak manufacturing-cost calculation.
A deployment decision should use at least three scenarios: low demand and elevated fault cost; realistic
recurring orders; and higher utilization with its required staffing and equipment. The evidence should
show what happens when the system is unavailable for several days, a material lot fails qualification or a

customer changes a specification after cutting. Reserves and service coverage should reflect those
events.

Two different economic conclusions
Under the reference supplier cost, the $100,000 selling price leaves little contribution even when the
operator model appears profitable. Under the optimized supplier cost, the machine business can have
room to cover fixed costs, but the operator still needs sufficient accepted sales and a competitive total
garment cost. Both models must work; one cannot be used as proof of the other.
The appropriate next costing artifact is a supplier-backed bill of materials and installation scope,
accompanied by a production-cost model populated with measured garment trials and real customer
orders. The numerical examples here make those questions explicit. They do not establish a profitable
product in advance of that evidence.



23 / Validation and product-development
sequence
The technical program should progress from the hardest unsupported transitions to complete garments,
then to repeatable mixed-order production. It should not begin by optimizing a visually attractive
enclosure or treating successful straight seams as proof of a full system. Several construction families
must influence the architecture before hardware choices make breadth prohibitively difficult.




Figure 16. Evidence-led development sequence. Each stage has measurable outputs; higher speed and broader materials require
separate qualification.


Stage 1: close the physical gaps
Produce a full support-and-access map for the reference shirt and representative woven and bulky-rib
operations. Build real-fabric rigs for extraction, an uncertain edge, a partly assembled garment, a collar or
cuff opening, and a turning or closure task. Demonstrate how the source releases and the destination
retains control. Compare mechanisms on realistic materials rather than selecting one from a polished
drawing.
The output is a chosen architecture with reasons, alternatives and identified limits. Necessary design
files include kinematics, tool interfaces, support geometry, sensing, safe-stop behavior and a tentative
cost allocation. A manual demonstration can help establish reach and access, but any hand movement
not provided by the proposed mechanisms remains an unresolved automation requirement.

Stage 2: connect an uninterrupted complete route
Integrate a defined garment recipe from loaded rolls through accepted or rejected output. Preserve
piece identities and material lots across cutting and sewing. Include rib-loop preparation, labels,
reinforcement, actual hems, turning and required final construction. Log every operator action. The
software should generate the same geometry and recipe used by the cell, not a separate demonstration
design.
One successful garment establishes an important integration result but not production reliability. The
cell may be slow and use costly prototype equipment at this stage; the report should identify which
components need redesign for the intended delivered product and why replacing them is technically
credible.

Stage 3: demonstrate repeatability and variation
Agree a test matrix before trials. Include repeated runs, size variations, changed material lots and
selected new constructions. Retain failures, reject garments and recovery logs. Assess output after
release and after the agreed offline wash and durability tests. A sample-specific adjustment performed
between every run must be disclosed; it is not evidence of a general recipe.
A cross-family test should use the same core transport and support architecture on meaningful knit,
woven and bulky-rib tasks. Document which changes were parameters, tools, material profiles or new
mechanisms. This makes “programmable” a measurable property rather than a marketing label.

Stage 4: measure complete shifts and economic performance
Run repeated 12-hour trials from declared starting conditions. Test both a repeated garment and a
mixed-order workload. Record accepted output, startup, pipeline draining, roll preparation, changeover,
consumed supplies, energy, paid operator time, automatic recoveries, manual interventions and quality
holds. Validate installation and service procedures on more than one physical build before claiming
repeatable deployment.
Compare measured costs with the supplier and operator models. Revise price, configuration, output
rating or product scope when evidence requires it. Do not retain an attractive target by excluding
inconvenient operations or changing the definition of an acceptable garment.

Required technical records
The final engineering record should include mechanical CAD, wiring, module interface descriptions,
safety analysis, calibration and maintenance procedures, recipe and policy versioning, a supplier-backed
costed parts list, process qualification, full trial data, software tests and known limitations. Customer-
facing documentation should state input conditions, setup, supported products, capacity basis, utilities,
service and exclusions in plain language.



24 / Conclusions and unresolved questions
The research and existing process equipment support a credible route toward an integrated, software-
directed garment-production cell. The proposed product does not require a new textile or a new
principle of stitch formation. It does require a substantial advance in connecting cutting, material control,
garment topology, sewing access and recoverable execution in a repeatable system.
The industrial format provides useful physical freedom: independent sewing processes, accessible
supports, parallel work and modular expansion. The AI contribution is meaningful when it reduces
uncertainty and setup effort, preserves design-to-production consistency or recovers from material

RESEARCH SNAPSHOT / 19 OCTOBER 2026                                                                       44
ROLL-TO-GARMENT / TECHNICAL AND COMMERCIAL WHITE PAPER



variation. AI does not remove physical constraints, replace all deterministic control or establish unlimited
designs.
Several relevant companies are pursuing parts of the same ambition, and some explicitly co-design
learning and mechanics. The differentiating case therefore rests on measured capabilities: conventional
stitch quality, end-to-end scope, useful garment breadth, transparent autonomy, low small-order
overhead, cost and serviceability. The paper does not establish a first-in-category claim.
The $100,000 selling-price objective is demanding. Under the illustrative reference supplier cost it
leaves almost no contribution, whereas an optimized build could leave materially more room. Garment-
operator profitability is similarly sensitive to utilization, material cost, yield and labor. The economic
models demonstrate what would need to be true, not what is already true.

Suggested trial record
Each attempt should record job ID, garment/recipe revision, material lot/profile, tool versions,

occurred and who performed it.

intended operating claim. Rare unattended failures need much larger evidence sets than one hundred
convenient cycles.

Example skill contract: transfer a joined upper body
The source holds a garment with completed shoulder seams. The target requires the neckline exposed
on an opening support with no unrelated body layer in the sewing path. The skill verifies identity, checks
source support, estimates pose, exposes the neckline, positions the receiving support, confirms
grip/layers, transfers control and only then releases the source.
Permitted recovery might re-observe, lift a known region or regrip within a limited tension envelope. A
twisted or unidentifiable garment goes to a reject/hold route or safe stop. Successful motion without
the target condition is not success. The result updates the garment state so the collar operation receives
a verified input rather than an assumption.

Procurement and engineering questions
Ask suppliers for exact input/output conditions, control interfaces, fault states, software access,
licensing, supported material limits, maintenance intervals and service lead times. Require full costs for
necessary air, vacuum, guarding and calibration. Clarify whether advertised cycle time includes loading,
cutting, offloading, cleaning and thread changes. Verify that the component can carry its gripper and
material at the required reach, not merely its nominal payload.
Ask pilot operators for matched samples, historical order quantities, setup time, reject and rework costs,
fabric and trim quotes, actual staffing, floor and utility costs, delivery expectations and willingness to
accept disclosed capability limits. This replaces speculative market enthusiasm with a testable process
and profit comparison.
